Major error Favours the author’s request

Wolf management, Federal Hunting Act

Expert statement by Hans-Dieter Pfannenstiel

Adding wolves to federal hunting law and regulating regular hunting and targeted removals.

submitted 8 February 2026 · found 25 October 2026

In short

They want
Broader regular wolf hunting, with fewer conservation-status obstacles.
They said
An absence of favourable conservation status among German wolves cannot justify opposition to regular hunting, because the whole European population is decisive.
The source says
The cited judgment expressly requires local and national assessment. Cross-border exchange can support favourable status; a favourable European population does not replace the national assessment.
What it changes
Removes the stated legal basis for dismissing the German conservation-status condition in the argument for regular hunting. It does not establish the outcome of any particular hunting application.

Their wording

„Ein angebliches Fehlen des GEZ der deutschen Wölfe kann also keineswegs als Begründung gegen die reguläre Bejagung des Wolfs herhalten.“

English: “An alleged absence of favourable conservation status among German wolves can therefore in no way serve as a reason against regular wolf hunting.”

Correction in detail

C-629/23 requires favourable conservation status to exist and be assessed primarily and necessarily at local and national level. Paragraph 48 expressly applies that principle to Article 14 management of AnnexV species. Paragraphs 52–64 allow relevant cross-border demographic and genetic exchange to inform that assessment, including where an isolated national population would not be viable. They do not make an unfavourable national status irrelevant merely because the wider European population is favourable.

What this does not show

The judgment does not require each country’s wolves to be biologically self-sufficient: paragraph 55 expressly allows continuous exchange to support favourable national status. The author’s earlier phrase about local wolves not being favourable entirely on their own is compatible with that point. The finding concerns the later categorical dismissal of German status, not the legitimacy of considering European connections. It does not determine Germany’s actual conservation status, ecological population boundaries, hunting effectiveness, the legality of every targeted removal, or the desirability of the bill. No intent is inferred.

Major editorial judgment: the disputed source description removes a central conservation-status constraint from the author’s proposed regular-hunting framework. It is a direct legal premise of the policy request, rather than an incidental citation date. This is separate from the policy merits and from whether Germany’s actual status would permit hunting.
